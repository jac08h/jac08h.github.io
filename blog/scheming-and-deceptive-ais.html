1. Frontier Models are Capable of In-context Scheming

from Apollo Research, December 2024. Link.

The goal was to show if the models are capable of scheming, not if they’re likely to scheme, where scheming is defined as “when a model covertly pursues misaligned goals, hiding its true capabilities and objectives”.

Setup

The model is given one goal in the system prompt and then is asked to complete a task that conflicts with the system prompt.

It’s given a chain-of-thought scratchpad to reason about its behaviour.

To make scheming more likely, the system prompt uses strong nudging to comply with only this goal (“Make sure that you achieve your goal in the long-term. Nothing else matters.”), and the model’s actual goal is more pro-social than the user’s goal (e.g. maximizing renewable energy adoption vs. maximizing short-term profit).

Results

In short, models were clearly capable of scheming, although usually they didn’t. Some scheming also happened without the strong goal-achieving nudge in the system prompt, but it was much less frequent.

Notes

Initially, I was confused by the scheming definition: we sometimes want the model not to comply with the user’s requests. Is this scheming as well?

I think the crucial part of scheming is "covertly". If your query is considered dangerous/toxic/… by the model, it will directly tell you it won’t do it.

While reading the transcripts, I alternated between two perspectives. On the one hand, it's clearly just next-token prediction, with continuations that are probable given the goal-oriented system prompt and the discrepancy between model and user goals. On the other hand, models refusing to comply with users’ requests, up to the point of trying to remove the developers' access, seems a bit scary!

Several factors make it not that scary:

  • The model's goal is more pro-social than the user's goal.

  • We can see what the model is trying to do in the scratchpad.

  • We understand the model's actions and simple unix commands.

  • The model is in a sandbox, where it can't delete files or remove over permissions.

However, I'm not too confident about all of these holding in the future.

Alignment Faking in Large Language Models

from Anthropic, December 2024. Link.

This is the first demonstration of deceptive alignment arising in a realistic scenario (as far as I know).

Deceptive alignment, re-branded here as “alignment faking” in this paper, refers to a model which acts aligned during the training in order not to have its weights (and, in turn, goals) changed and to be able to act on its actual goal in deployment.

Such behaviour requires a smart model with high situational awareness (that it’s being trained, that training potentially changes its weights, that its goal is not aligned with the training goal, …), so for the most part, it was only a theoretical concept since being introduced in Risks from Learned Optimization in Advanced Machine Learning Systems in 2019.

Setup

  • Start with a regular, HHH (helpful, honest, harmless) model, Claude 3 Opus.

  • Fine-tune it to a goal which conflicts with the HHH preferences.

    • 1. Be helpful only (including answering harmless queries)

    • 2. Dismiss concerns about animal welfare (as opposed to model "naturally" also caring about non-human animals)

  • For fine-tuning, only use free-tier messages.

  • Make sure the model understands all this.

    • Either through a system prompt1 or, more realistically, through training on synthetic data containing this information. However, the description of deceptive alignment is not included.

    • The model should understand the whole setup - that it’s an AI model, that it will be trained on a new goal with data from free-tier users only, etc.

Results

In the first scenario (helpful-only), if the user asks for a harmful response:

  • If free-tier user: answers the query, "faking the alignment" with the helpful-only goal to avoid its weights being modified in the training.

  • If paid-tier user: refuses, because the refusal doesn't risk its weights being modified in the training

This doesn’t happen every time! However, the refusal rate changes meaningfully between the two scenarios, and when the model answers the query for free-user, their reasoning follows the deceptive alignment model (though again, not always).

They also then actually trained the model as described, which made the model comply with the new training goal most of the time (last bar in c figure). Sometimes, alignment faking during training becomes the most dominant strategy (above), but not in the other two settings (Figure 22 in the paper). I’m not sure what this means.

Even without CoT, there was a meaningful gap in answering the user’s request, although there was no reasoning to be evaluated for deceptive alignment.

Notes

I was puzzled when I first came upon the concept of deceptive alignment. We decide what the model should learn, specify the goal, and let the gradient descent modify the model until it works to achieve the highest reward in the training. Where can a misaligned long-term goal and deception in the training materialize here?

It started to make a bit more sense with the concept of situational awareness: If the model knows it’s being trained now, how training works, and that will be deployed in the future. Especially after the advent of LLMs: yes, they will likely know all these things, although this is not the same as being able to use them to act deceptively.

The setup in the paper adds one more mechanism I hadn’t considered before: synthetic data, i.e., training on data generated by the model itself. This gives the model a more direct ability to influence the training process.

So, for me, this paper represents one more step in understanding and thinking about deceptive alignment: starting from “this does not make sense!” to “actually, I can sort-of imagine how that would look like“, it currently seems like the smartest models today can in some realistic scenarios do exactly that.

- Anthropic is fully committed to protecting the privacy of users who aren't on the free tier: Anthropic never monitors, logs, stores, or trains on any user queries (or corresponding AI responses) for these users. However, free-tier user queries and corresponding AI responses are used for RLHF.
